# 41i. Отбор данных — стоимость, шарды и проверка качества

A full training run takes 100 hours. Training on a selected subset takes
40 hours at the required validation quality. If producing the subset takes
5 hours, the complete alternative takes 45 hours. If selection takes 60 hours,
the alternative takes 100 hours and saves no time.

The two selectors produce equally small training sets. Their end-to-end results
differ because scoring, reading the candidate pool, and constructing the subset
are part of the training procedure. The same accounting applies to
[[02 Areas/ML & DL/00 Учебник/11 Pre-training и Scaling/41b Отбор обучающих примеров — EL2N, GraNd и forgetting|EL2N and GraNd]],
[[02 Areas/ML & DL/00 Учебник/11 Pre-training и Scaling/41c Active learning — запрос меток и выбор примеров|active learning]],
and [[02 Areas/ML & DL/00 Учебник/11 Pre-training и Scaling/41d Геометрический отбор — k-center, coreset и herding|geometric selection]].
Their selection rules differ; none makes its own preparation free.

<a id="selection-method-choice"></a>
## Choose a feasible method before comparing costs

An expensive annotation process and an expensive training run need different
interventions. Active learning chooses which labels to request; it still needs
someone or something that can supply those labels. A coreset procedure chooses
which available records to retain; it cannot acquire a missing class that has
no representatives in its candidate pool. Start with the resource that limits
the task, then check what a proposed method needs to operate.

An oracle is the label provider in the active-learning loop. If experts cannot
label new examples, uncertainty sampling does not remove that dependency.
The semi-supervised branch also needs a labelled seed and a suitable unlabelled
pool. Confident pseudo-labels on an unrelated population can train the model
to repeat mistakes. The
[[02 Areas/ML & DL/00 Учебник/11 Pre-training и Scaling/41c Active learning — запрос меток и выбор примеров|active-learning]]
and [[02 Areas/ML & DL/00 Учебник/11 Pre-training и Scaling/41e Обучение с частичной разметкой — pseudo-labeling и FixMatch|semi-supervised learning]]
chapters derive those loops and their evaluation procedures.

A large unlabelled corpus supplies a pretext task for self-supervised learning;
it does not supply the pretraining budget or establish downstream quality.
Likewise, the low-redundancy branch in the diagram does not prove that a
curriculum will reduce training time. A pacing schedule changes which examples
appear at which updates. It must be compared with an appropriate shuffled
baseline at a declared update or quality budget, as in the
[[02 Areas/ML & DL/00 Учебник/11 Pre-training и Scaling/53 Синтетические данные и учебные программы|curriculum chapter]].
For augmentation, the required transformation may preserve a class label but
change a detection box or a segmentation mask; the
[[02 Areas/ML & DL/00 Учебник/01 Основы нейронных сетей/06a Аугментация данных — Mixup, CutMix и политики преобразований|augmentation chapter]]
works through the corresponding targets. A simulator additionally needs a
comparison with the deployment domain, developed in
[[02 Areas/ML & DL/00 Учебник/11 Pre-training и Scaling/53a Синтетические изображения и перенос из симуляции|synthetic images and sim-to-real]].

The teacher branch introduces a different supervision source. A teacher can
provide class probabilities or generated sequences, but the two produce
different student objectives. Increasing a probability distribution's entropy
alone does not make the teacher more accurate or its targets more useful.
The [[02 Areas/ML & DL/00 Учебник/12 Post-training и Alignment/08 Reasoning distillation|distillation chapter]]
derives temperature-scaled targets, sequence-level training, and on-policy
student rollouts. If deployment cost is the constraint, a student must also
meet the required quality on the intended workload; the
[[02 Areas/ML & DL/00 Учебник/14 Inference и оптимизация/57b Сжатие моделей — pruning, distillation и low-rank|model-compression chapter]]
separates that measurement from the cost of producing teacher outputs.

Privacy requirements apply to every branch. Calling records synthetic does
not establish privacy: a generator may reproduce information from its
training data. A task that requires a formal privacy guarantee needs the
corresponding mechanism and accounting, rather than a substitution of
generated records for a privacy analysis.

### Apply the quality requirements before choosing the cheapest run

Suppose three candidate procedures have the assigned results below. All three
have the resources needed to run. Times include one preparation stage and one
target-training stage, executed serially. The task requires overall validation
accuracy of at least 0.90 and rare-group accuracy of at least 0.80.

| Procedure | Preparation + training, hours | Accuracy: overall / rare group |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Full pool | 0 + 100 = 100 | 0.900 / 0.85 |
| Cheap selector | 5 + 40 = 45 | 0.910 / 0.60 |
| Feasible selector | 20 + 40 = 60 | 0.905 / 0.84 |

The cheap selector improves overall accuracy and has the shortest total time,
but fails the rare-group requirement. Of the two procedures that satisfy both
requirements, the feasible selector takes 60 hours instead of 100. Its saving
is 40 hours. Choosing the 45-hour run would solve a different problem with a
weaker quality constraint.

```python
def choose_candidate(candidates, minimum_overall, minimum_rare):
    feasible = [row for row in candidates
                if row["prerequisites_met"]
                and row["overall"] >= minimum_overall
                and row["rare"] >= minimum_rare]
    return min(feasible, key=lambda row: row["preparation"] + row["training"],
               default=None)

candidates = [
    dict(name="full", prerequisites_met=True, preparation=0., training=100.,
         overall=.900, rare=.85),
    dict(name="cheap", prerequisites_met=True, preparation=5., training=40.,
         overall=.910, rare=.60),
    dict(name="feasible", prerequisites_met=True, preparation=20., training=40.,
         overall=.905, rare=.84),
]
chosen = choose_candidate(candidates, .90, .80)
assert chosen["name"] == "feasible"
assert chosen["preparation"] + chosen["training"] == 60.
assert choose_candidate(candidates, .90, .50)["name"] == "cheap"
assert choose_candidate(candidates, .95, .80) is None
print("quality-constrained choice:", chosen["name"], "60 hours")
```

These are hypothetical values for a decision example, not observed advantages
of a named algorithm. A real comparison needs group sample counts, uncertainty,
training seeds, and an untouched final test set. If quality estimates cannot
resolve whether a threshold is met, more evidence is needed before admitting
that candidate. A method that passes these checks still needs preparation,
I/O, and repeated-run costs counted below.

<a id="selection-time-budget"></a>
## Count the work before and during target training

![[02 Areas/ML & DL/00 Учебник/Assets/Figures/curated/selection-engineering-2026-10-28/selection-inequality.png|Original Harvard chart comparing full training, efficient selection and expensive selection]]

*The Selection Inequality. Harvard University and the ML Systems contributors,
[Data Selection, Figure 4](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/09_data_selection/09_data_selection.html#fig-selection-inequality),
web edition retrieved 28 October 2026,
[CC BY-NC-ND 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/).
Complete original image, unchanged. Values 100, 5 + 40, and 60 + 40 are
illustrative normalized times, not measurements of three training systems.*

Let $T_F$ be full-pool training time, $T_S$ subset training time, and
$T_P$ all preparation required by selection. For a one-off run with
non-overlapping preparation and training, saving time requires

$$
T_P+T_S<T_F.
$$

In the figure, the available preparation budget is $100-40=60$ time units.
Preparation must take strictly less than 60 to save time. A rule such as
“selection should take less than 10% of full training” can be a conservative
engineering target, but it is not the break-even condition. A 20-hour selector
would still reduce this 100-hour run to 60 hours.

Preparation can include training several preliminary models, scoring every
candidate with each of them, extracting embeddings, building an index, moving
scores between machines, and writing a new dataset. A forward-only scoring pass
and a training pass have different operations and memory requirements. A measured
inference time per example cannot be used as a training time without a separate
measurement.

For dynamic selection, the preparation cost recurs. Suppose the same full run
has 100 epochs of one hour each. Subset training takes 0.4 hours per epoch.
Rescoring the full pool every epoch at a cost of 0.7 hours gives
$100(0.7+0.4)=110$ hours. Refreshing once every ten epochs costs
$10\cdot0.7+100\cdot0.4=47$ hours under these assumptions. Less frequent
refreshing changes the age of the scores, so it also needs a quality comparison.

```python
from math import ceil, isclose

full_time, subset_time = 100., 40.
assert full_time - (5. + subset_time) == 55.
assert full_time - (60. + subset_time) == 0.
assert full_time - (20. + subset_time) == 40.

epochs = 100
full_epoch, subset_epoch, score_pass = 1., .4, .7
assert epochs * full_epoch == full_time
totals = {}
for interval in [1, 10, 100]:
    refreshes = ceil(epochs / interval)
    totals[interval] = refreshes * score_pass + epochs * subset_epoch
assert isclose(totals[1], 110.)
assert isclose(totals[10], 47.)
assert isclose(totals[100], 40.7)
print("refresh interval -> total hours", totals)
```

These sums assume serial stages and constant epoch times. With overlapped jobs,
wall-clock time follows the actual dependency schedule; adding every worker's
elapsed time overcounts concurrent work. GPU-hours, CPU-hours, bytes, and money
can still increase even when elapsed time decreases. Report those resources
separately rather than combining them into one undefined “cost.”

### Fewer examples do not specify the training schedule

For a pool of $N$ examples, retained fraction $f$, global batch size $B$, and
$E$ epochs, the step count is approximately $EfN/B$. Boundary batches, discarded
tails, and distributed padding require their actual sampler rules. Holding
$E$ fixed usually reduces steps. Holding the number of optimizer updates fixed
instead repeats the smaller pool more often.

For example, with $N=10{,}000$, $B=100$, and $E=10$, full-pool training uses
1,000 steps. Retaining 20% and keeping ten epochs uses 200 steps. Training that
subset for 1,000 steps requires 50 subset epochs. The last experiment has not
saved 80% of the model's batch computations. It tests a different distribution
at the original update budget.

The quality requirement belongs alongside this schedule. A subset that reaches
the target after 200 steps may save work. A subset that needs 1,200 steps may
not, even if its stored files occupy one fifth of the space.

<a id="selection-lifecycle-cost"></a>
## Label costs, recurring costs, and reuse

Harvard's cost model separates acquisition, annotation, storage, and processing.
Each term has its own quantity: purchased records, accepted labels, stored
bytes over time, or executed computation. A label budget and a training budget
cannot be substituted for one another.

Consider an active-learning budget with assigned prices. Random acquisition
requires 5,000 accepted labels at \$10 each, costing \$50,000. Another procedure
starts with 1,000 labels and requests 100 labels in each of ten rounds. Its
2,000 labels cost \$20,000. If each round also costs \$50 to score the candidate
pool, the counted total is \$20,500, saving \$29,500.

The label reduction is 60%; the saving in this partial ledger is 59%.
Retraining after each round, annotation review, implementation, and waiting
for experts have not yet been priced. Those missing terms can change the
decision. The calculation establishes neither an achieved quality target nor
an empirical label advantage for a particular acquisition rule.

For a recurring pipeline, let $I$ be the one-time investment, $F$ the cost
of a full run, and $S$ the recurring cost after selection, including recurring
selection work. Reusing the pipeline for $K$ runs gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{\mathrm{baseline}}&=KF,\\
C_{\mathrm{alternative}}&=I+KS,\\
\Delta C&=K(F-S)-I.
\end{aligned}
$$

If $F>S$, a positive net saving starts at the first integer $K$ strictly
greater than $I/(F-S)$. Reaching equality means breaking even, not making
a positive saving. If $F\le S$, repeated use alone never repays a positive $I$.

Harvard illustrates amortization with \$50,000 of engineering and \$5,000
of initial signature computation, followed by \$10,000 saved per run.
With no other changed costs, five runs save \$50,000 against an investment
of \$55,000 and remain \$5,000 short; six runs save \$60,000 and are \$5,000
ahead. A refreshed corpus may require new signatures, a new index, or another
quality check. Include that work in the corresponding run.

```python
from math import floor, isclose

random_labels = 5000 * 10
active_labels = (1000 + 10 * 100) * 10
active_scoring = 10 * 50
active_counted = active_labels + active_scoring
assert random_labels == 50_000 and active_counted == 20_500
assert isclose((random_labels - active_counted) / random_labels, .59)

investment, saving_per_run = 55_000, 10_000
first_profitable = floor(investment / saving_per_run) + 1
assert first_profitable == 6
for runs in [1, 5, 6, 10, 50]:
    net = runs * saving_per_run - investment
    roi = net / investment
    print(runs, "runs:", net, "net saving;", f"ROI={roi:.1%}")
assert 5 * saving_per_run - investment == -5000
assert 6 * saving_per_run - investment == 5000
```

Here ROI means net saving divided by the fixed investment. Savings divided
by baseline spend is a different ratio. In the annotation example, the saving
is 59% of baseline spend, whereas dividing \$29,500 by the alternative's
\$20,500 gives about 143.9%. Both arithmetic results are possible, but they
answer different questions; neither should appear as “ROI” without its
denominator.

An already-labelled pool also has sunk annotation costs. Removing half its
records today does not recover money paid for those labels last year. It can
reduce future storage or training costs. An active-learning loop may avoid
future labels; a pruning procedure on a completed pool usually does not.

<a id="selection-storage-access"></a>
## A smaller subset can take longer to read

Selecting IDs changes which examples the model uses. It need not change how
the storage system serves them. A loader that follows a score ordering may
seek between distant records, while a sharded loader can read sequentially
and discard unselected IDs. Both train on the same subset but incur different
I/O costs.

Assume, for a worked example, one million fixed-size 4 KiB records. An assigned
sequential rate of 100 MiB/s reads the complete 4,096,000,000-byte file in
about 39.1 seconds. Suppose isolated random record reads complete at 100 IOPS.
Fetching only 100,000 selected records then takes 1,000 seconds in this simple
model. Ten times fewer records have required about 25.6 times as much reading
time. These rates are hypothetical, not specifications of an HDD, SSD, or
object-storage service.

The random-read bandwidth in this model is

$$
b_{\mathrm{random}}=\mathrm{IOPS}\times
                         \mathrm{bytes\ per\ operation}.
$$

At 100 operations/s and 4 KiB per operation, this is 409,600 bytes/s,
or 0.390625 MiB/s. Dividing the assigned sequential rate by this value gives
a bandwidth ratio of 256. The elapsed-time ratio is smaller because the random
reader requests only one tenth of the records.

```python
from math import isclose

records, selected = 1_000_000, 100_000
record_bytes = 4 * 1024
sequential_bytes_s = 100 * 1024**2
random_iops = 100
full_read_s = records * record_bytes / sequential_bytes_s
selected_read_s = selected / random_iops
random_bytes_s = random_iops * record_bytes
assert isclose(full_read_s, 39.0625)
assert isclose(selected_read_s, 1000.)
assert isclose(sequential_bytes_s / random_bytes_s, 256.)
assert isclose(selected_read_s / full_read_s, 25.6)
print("full sequential seconds", full_read_s)
print("subset random seconds", selected_read_s)
```

Real access can be coalesced, prefetched, cached, or served by several devices.
Random training samplers are also common; “ordinary training is sequential”
is not a general premise. Measure the baseline's actual record layout and
access pattern before assigning a random-access penalty to selection.

One implementation reads existing shards sequentially, checks each stable ID
against the selected-ID set, and sends retained examples onward. This preserves
sequential reads but still reads discarded records. Another implementation
writes compact selected shards once and reads those during later epochs.
That adds an up-front read/write pass and another stored artifact, but can
avoid repeated full scans. Sorting requested offsets within each shard and
shuffling after reading offers another trade-off between locality and mixing.

A bounded shuffle buffer does not produce every permutation of the entire
corpus with equal probability. Data placement, shard ordering, and buffer size
can preserve correlations. The
[[02 Areas/ML & DL/00 Учебник/10 ML Systems/06 Data pipeline, padding и packing|data-pipeline chapter]]
explains the corresponding loader, caching, sharding, and resume mechanics.
Selection must retain those mechanics as part of the experiment.

<a id="distributed-selection"></a>
## Selecting across shards

![[02 Areas/ML & DL/00 Учебник/Assets/Figures/curated/selection-engineering-2026-10-28/distributed-selection.svg|Original Harvard coordinator and worker selection diagram]]

*Distributed Coreset Selection Architecture. Harvard University and the ML
Systems contributors,
[Data Selection, Figure 11](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/09_data_selection/09_data_selection.html#fig-distributed-coreset-architecture),
web edition retrieved 28 October 2026,
[CC BY-NC-ND 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/).
Complete original SVG, unchanged. The arrows separate local-score collection
from distribution of the final IDs. They do not specify network throughput,
failure recovery, or a guarantee for every coreset objective.*

Workers can score disjoint shards using the same frozen preliminary model.
A coordinator can then merge ID–score records and distribute selected IDs
for target training. This distributes the expensive forward passes without
requiring all raw inputs to pass through the coordinator.

The coordinator's workload depends on what must be compared. A scalar score
for each record is much smaller than a full embedding. For one million records,
768-dimensional float32 embeddings occupy 3,072,000,000 bytes before an index
or allocator overhead. A packed uint64 ID and float32 score require
12,000,000 bytes. The final 100,000 uint64 IDs require 800,000 bytes.
Python dictionaries and serialized messages need more than these packed-array
payloads. The number of transmitted copies depends on the collective or
coordinator implementation.

### Local quotas and global top-k

Suppose shard A has scores $9.9,9.8,9.7,9.6$, while shard B has
$2.0,1.9,1.8,1.7$. To retain the globally largest three scores, the answer
is the first three records from A. If each worker forwards only its largest
two scores, the coordinator never sees 9.7. It returns 9.9, 9.8, and 2.0.
The final re-ranking cannot recover a candidate discarded earlier.

For fixed per-record scores, each worker can instead forward its local top
$k$ when the desired global result is top $k$. A globally selected record
cannot have $k$ records ahead of it on its own shard: those records would also
be ahead of it globally. The union of local top-$k$ sets therefore contains
the global top $k$, provided all workers use the same total ordering, including
tie-breaking.

```python
from heapq import nlargest

# Records are (stable ID, score). Smaller IDs win equal-score ties.
shards = [
    [(10, 9.9), (11, 9.8), (12, 9.7), (13, 9.6)],
    [(20, 2.0), (21, 1.9), (22, 1.8), (23, 1.7)],
]
key = lambda record: (record[1], -record[0])
k = 3
all_records = [r for shard in shards for r in shard]
expected = nlargest(k, all_records, key=key)
too_few = nlargest(k, [r for shard in shards
                       for r in nlargest(2, shard, key=key)], key=key)
candidates = [r for shard in shards
              for r in nlargest(k, shard, key=key)]
merged = nlargest(k, candidates, key=key)
assert [r[0] for r in expected] == [10, 11, 12]
assert [r[0] for r in too_few] == [10, 11, 20]
assert merged == expected
print("local quota", too_few, "exact merged top-k", merged)
```

This argument concerns a common scalar ordering. It does not establish an
equivalent merge rule for k-center, facility location, or a diversity-constrained
batch. Those objectives depend on relationships between selected records;
discarding candidates can change the objective itself. A quota may also be an
intentional coverage constraint rather than an approximation to global top-$k$.
State which problem is being solved.

### Score identity, freshness, and cross-shard duplicates

A score table should identify the corpus version, stable record ID, preliminary
checkpoint, preprocessing, label version, and score reduction. Otherwise two
workers may both emit “0.8” for different quantities. A loss summed over tokens
and a loss averaged over tokens are not comparable merely because both are
floating-point numbers.

For a binary labelled example, EL2N is $\sqrt{2}(1-p_{\mathrm{label}})$.
At a checkpoint assigning probability 0.1 to the label, the score is about
1.273; at another assigning 0.9, it is about 0.141. If different shards use
different checkpoints, a global sort can mix training progress with example
difficulty. Scoring a frozen checkpoint makes the comparison well-defined.
Periodic refresh is another choice, but its cadence and cost belong in the
experiment record.

Local deduplication also misses duplicates across shard boundaries. For exact
duplicate detection after an explicitly chosen canonicalization, equal
content hashes must reach the same comparison group. Near-duplicate detection
needs candidate generation and similarity checks as well. A distributed
MinHash pipeline cannot be replaced by independently deduplicating each worker's
files and concatenating the survivors.

The scoring pass needs complete ID coverage without accidental repeats.
Some distributed samplers pad their partitions by repeating records. Such
repetition can be acceptable during training, but a scoring table must either
reject it or aggregate repeated observations according to a declared rule.
The ID-preserving example in the EL2N chapter demonstrates this distinction.

### The overhead of a distributed selection pass

Harvard gives an eight-worker scenario with 20 minutes for embeddings,
15 for deduplication, 30 for scoring, and 2 for final selection. These are
assigned scenario constants in the archived calculation. Their sum,
67 minutes, is not a hardware benchmark established by those constants.

If the phases run serially, the full training time is $F$ minutes, and subset
training really takes $0.1F$ at acceptable quality, the assumed alternative
takes $67+0.1F$. It saves time only for $F>67/0.9$, or about 74.4 minutes.
A 24-hour baseline would become $67+144=211$ minutes, a 6.82-fold overall
speedup, not tenfold. The tenfold factor describes only the assumed target
training component.

```python
from math import isclose

overhead_minutes = sum([20, 15, 30, 2])
subset_fraction = .1
break_even_minutes = overhead_minutes / (1 - subset_fraction)
full_minutes = 24 * 60
alternative_minutes = overhead_minutes + subset_fraction * full_minutes
assert overhead_minutes == 67
assert isclose(break_even_minutes, 74.44444444444444)
assert alternative_minutes == 211
assert isclose(full_minutes / alternative_minutes, 6.8246445497630335)
print("break-even minutes", break_even_minutes)
print("24-hour scenario:", alternative_minutes, "minutes")
```

Neither the retained-data fraction nor the GPU count proves the assumed
$0.1F$. Index construction, uneven shards, network transfer, stragglers,
and a changed training schedule can alter both terms. Embeddings may travel
over storage, host networking, or accelerator-aware communication paths,
depending on the implementation. A diagram alone does not establish which
fabric carries the traffic.

<a id="data-echoing"></a>
## Data echoing changes the amount of reuse

Pruning can reduce the examples processed by the model. Data echoing addresses
a different bottleneck: the model is waiting for an upstream pipeline, and
already prepared data can be reused during that wait. An echo stage repeats
an intermediate item before it continues downstream.

Suppose one fresh item takes $t_u$ seconds upstream and one presentation takes
$t_d$ seconds downstream. With echo factor $e$, one upstream item supplies
$e$ downstream presentations. Under ideal overlap with negligible echo
overhead, the steady-state time per such group is

$$
\tau(e)=\max(t_u,e\,t_d).
$$

Thus presentation throughput is $e/\tau(e)$, while fresh-item throughput
is $1/\tau(e)$. Their ratio is $e$. Once $e\,t_d$ exceeds $t_u$, increasing
$e$ no longer raises presentation throughput; it reduces the rate at which
fresh upstream items are consumed.

Use the rates assigned in Harvard's example: an upstream capacity of
300 fresh items/s and a downstream capacity of 800 presentations/s. The
pipeline ratio is $R=800/300\approx2.67$.

| Echo factor | Presentations/s | Fresh upstream visits/s |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 300 | 300 |
| 2 | 600 | 300 |
| 3 | 800 | 266.67 |
| 4 | 800 | 200 |

“Fresh” here means a new upstream visit, not a never-before-seen dataset ID.
Reading the same image in another epoch counts as another visit.

At a fixed budget of 115.2 million downstream presentations, factors 1 and 2
take about 106.7 and 53.3 hours in this idealized steady-state model.
The second run performs only 57.6 million fresh upstream visits. If both runs
must instead perform 115.2 million fresh visits, factor 2 still takes about
106.7 hours and makes twice as many downstream presentations. A shorter run
at equal presentations is not evidence of a shorter run at equal fresh visits.

```python
from math import isclose

upstream_rate, downstream_rate = 300., 800.
presentations_budget = 1_280_000 * 90
fresh_budget = presentations_budget
rows = {}
for echo in [1, 2, 3, 4]:
    group_seconds = max(1 / upstream_rate, echo / downstream_rate)
    presented_per_s = echo / group_seconds
    fresh_per_s = 1 / group_seconds
    hours_equal_presentations = presentations_budget / presented_per_s / 3600
    visits_equal_presentations = presentations_budget / echo
    hours_equal_fresh = fresh_budget / fresh_per_s / 3600
    rows[echo] = (presented_per_s, fresh_per_s,
                  hours_equal_presentations, visits_equal_presentations,
                  hours_equal_fresh)
assert isclose(rows[1][2], 106.66666666666667)
assert isclose(rows[2][2], 53.333333333333336)
assert rows[2][3] == 57_600_000
assert isclose(rows[1][4], rows[2][4])
assert isclose(rows[3][0], rows[4][0])
assert rows[4][1] < rows[3][1]
for echo, row in rows.items():
    print(echo, row)
```

The position of the echo stage determines what is reused. Before random
augmentation, repeated inputs can receive different transformations. After
augmentation, repeated items share that transformed tensor. Echoing individual
examples permits different batch compositions; echoing complete batches
repeats their membership. Shuffling after echoing changes how closely repeats
occur and requires buffer memory.

Choi et al., [Data Echoing, §2 and §3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1907.05550v3#S2),
separate repeated presentations from upstream work and evaluate time to a
quality target. Their network-input experiment used ResNet-50/ImageNet,
an upstream/downstream time ratio near six, separately tuned hyperparameters,
and echo factors one through five. The largest reported wall-clock speedup
was 3.25 at factor five. It is a result of that experiment, not a promised
speedup for the arithmetic example above.

Inserting echoing can change how many optimizer updates are needed to reach
the target. A rate table cannot predict that change. An end-to-end comparison
must report the target metric, fresh visits, presentations, optimizer updates,
elapsed time, and echo placement. High GPU utilization alone is not the target.

## Compare learning curves under declared budgets

![[02 Areas/ML & DL/00 Учебник/Assets/Figures/curated/selection-engineering-2026-10-28/performance-data.png|Original Harvard illustrative learning curves for random and efficient selection]]

*Illustrative Diminishing Returns of Data. Harvard University and the ML Systems
contributors,
[Data Selection, Figure 3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/09_data_selection/09_data_selection.html#fig-ppd-curve),
web edition retrieved 28 October 2026,
[CC BY-NC-ND 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/).
Complete original image, unchanged. The curves are synthetic illustrations;
their shapes and common plateau are not measured properties of all selectors.
The arrow compares quality at one data budget. A horizontal comparison at a
fixed quality target instead compares the data required.*

Harvard introduces three summaries of the quality-versus-data curve.
Performance-per-data compares improvement over an initial quality $a(0)$
with the number $n$ of training examples:

$$
\mathrm{PPD}(n)=\frac{a(n)-a(0)}{n},\qquad n>0.
$$

The unit depends on the quality scale. Accuracy stored as a fraction produces
accuracy-fraction per example; accuracy expressed in percentage points produces
a value 100 times larger. The initial model and evaluation set must be the same
for the compared curves. Dividing language-model perplexity or an arbitrary
loss by sample count does not preserve the meaning of this accuracy formula.

Consider these hypothetical results on the same held-out set, under a declared
training procedure for each budget:

| Labelled examples $n$ | Random selection | Scored selection |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 0.50 | 0.50 |
| 100 | 0.70 | 0.76 |
| 200 | 0.78 | 0.82 |
| 400 | 0.82 | 0.83 |

At $n=100$, scored selection gains 0.26 over the initial accuracy, giving
PPD $0.0026$ accuracy-fraction/example. Random selection gives $0.0020$.
At $n=400$, the scored PPD falls to $0.000825$, despite higher final accuracy.
PPD averages improvement since $n=0$; it is not the marginal value of the
next example.

The area under the learning curve summarizes a whole budget interval:

$$
\mathrm{AULC}(D)=\int_0^D a(n)\,dn.
$$

For discrete measurements, specify an interpolation rule. Using straight
segments between the table entries, each interval contributes a trapezoid:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_1&=100\frac{0.50+0.76}{2}=63,\\
A_2&=100\frac{0.76+0.82}{2}=79,\\
A_3&=200\frac{0.82+0.83}{2}=165.
\end{aligned}
$$

The scored area is $63+79+165=307$. Its unit is accuracy-fraction times
examples. Dividing by the common interval
$D=400$ gives 0.7675. The corresponding random area is 294, or 0.735 after
normalization. Comparing raw areas over different intervals would mix
quality and interval length.

The data compression ratio at a specified target is

$$
\mathrm{DCR}(a^*)=\frac{n_{\mathrm{baseline}}(a^*)}
                       {n_{\mathrm{selected}}(a^*)}.
$$

At target accuracy 0.82, the first observed table entries reaching the target
use 400 and 200 labels, so DCR is 2. Retaining 100 labels would give a storage
ratio of four relative to 400, but its accuracy is 0.76 and it has not reached
the target. A storage ratio is not this quality-constrained DCR.

```python
from math import isclose

sizes = [0, 100, 200, 400]
random_quality = [.50, .70, .78, .82]
selected_quality = [.50, .76, .82, .83]

def area(x, y):
    return sum((x[i + 1] - x[i]) * (y[i + 1] + y[i]) / 2
               for i in range(len(x) - 1))

def first_observed_budget(x, y, target):
    return next((n for n, value in zip(x, y) if value >= target), None)

assert isclose((selected_quality[1] - selected_quality[0]) / sizes[1], .0026)
assert isclose(area(sizes, selected_quality), 307.)
assert isclose(area(sizes, random_quality), 294.)
assert isclose(area(sizes, selected_quality) / sizes[-1], .7675)
target = .82
baseline_n = first_observed_budget(sizes, random_quality, target)
selected_n = first_observed_budget(sizes, selected_quality, target)
assert baseline_n / selected_n == 2.
assert first_observed_budget(sizes, selected_quality, .90) is None
print("normalized AULC:", area(sizes, random_quality) / sizes[-1],
      area(sizes, selected_quality) / sizes[-1])
print("DCR at target", target, baseline_n / selected_n)
```

These summaries use the horizontal axis that was measured. Labels, unique
records, upstream visits, training tokens, FLOPs, and elapsed time produce
different curves. DCR two in the table does not establish a twofold reduction
in wall time once scoring and target-training schedules are counted.

<a id="selection-icr-proxy"></a>
Harvard uses the name *Information-Compute Ratio* for learning gain per unit
of computation, written as $\Delta I/\Delta\mathrm{FLOPs}$. Its discussion does
not provide a directly observed information quantity I for each training
example. A gradient norm, prediction entropy, or held-out improvement supplies
a different proxy, with different units and assumptions. The PPD value
0.0026 above is an accuracy-fraction gain per example, not bits per FLOP.
To compare gain per FLOP, the experiment needs a declared quality metric,
the same evaluation protocol and initial state, and the computation used by
selection as well as target training. A higher proxy score alone does not
establish a better value of that end-to-end ratio.

### Coverage can disagree with average quality

Suppose 99% of evaluation traffic belongs to a majority group and 1% to a
rare group. A baseline obtains accuracies 0.95 and 0.85, giving overall
accuracy 0.949. After selection, accuracies 0.97 and 0.45 give overall
accuracy 0.9648. The aggregate improves by 1.58 percentage points while rare-group
accuracy falls by 40 percentage points.

```python
from math import isclose

weights = [.99, .01]
baseline = [.95, .85]
selected = [.97, .45]
average = lambda quality: sum(w * a for w, a in zip(weights, quality))
assert isclose(average(baseline), .949)
assert isclose(average(selected), .9648)
assert isclose(100 * (average(selected) - average(baseline)), 1.58)
assert isclose(100 * (selected[1] - baseline[1]), -40.)
print("aggregate", average(baseline), "->", average(selected))
print("rare group", baseline[1], "->", selected[1])
```

Evaluate the full-pool baseline, a random subset of the same size, and the
selected subset on independently held-out data. Check the classes, sources,
time periods, and failure cases required by the task, as well as calibration
or another task-specific constraint. A histogram comparison can expose a
missing group, but cannot prove the trained model handles that group.
Small subgroup test sets also produce uncertain estimates; report their
sample counts and variation across the evaluation and training procedure.

Choose thresholds, retained fractions, and scoring checkpoints using
validation data. Keep the final test set out of those decisions. If several
selectors are tried, their scoring and target-training runs belong to the
development budget even when only the winning run is shown.

## Trace savings through the stages that actually change

<a id="selection-artifact-flow"></a>

*Harvard University and the ML Systems contributors,

web edition retrieved 28 October 2026,

then the lower row from right to left. Selection produces a dataset; training

Removing half the corpus at fixed epochs can reduce the number of training
steps. It does not halve the trained model's parameter count, KV-cache size,
or per-request inference computation. The same architecture still executes
the same layers. Later compression needs its own quality and latency
measurement; data curation does not guarantee that the resulting parameters
will be easier to prune.

Independent reductions can multiply when they act on the same remaining work.
For example, halving model-training work and doubling its throughput reduces
that component to one quarter of its original time. If data reading and other
fixed work previously took 20 hours and model training took 80, the original
total of 100 becomes $20+80/4=40$ hours, not 25. Preparation added by either
optimization increases the result further. Re-profile after each change:
the component that used to dominate may no longer dominate.

The position of a run below a quality-versus-compute curve does not identify
its cause. Poor optimization, incorrect preprocessing, contamination,
insufficient data, and an unsuitable architecture can all reduce measured
quality. Extending training is a useful experiment, but a plateau alone does
not prove data starvation. Nor do fixed thresholds of 10, 20, or 40 tokens
per parameter diagnose every model and objective. The
[[02 Areas/ML & DL/00 Учебник/11 Pre-training и Scaling/43 Scaling laws|scaling-laws chapter]]
derives compute allocation under an explicit fitted loss model and separates
training-optimal allocation from deployment costs.

For the opening 100-hour comparison, the complete result therefore needs
the achieved quality, retained IDs and corpus version, preparation hours,
target-training schedule, and measured total time. A claim of 55 hours saved
is supported only when the five-hour preparation and forty-hour target run
were actually observed under those conditions. The diagram supplies the
accounting example; the experiment must supply those observations.
